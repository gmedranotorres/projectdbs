Turning messy records into clearer decisions

Gabriela Medrano Torres, M.S. candidate in Computational Analysis and Public Policy, Harris School of Public Policy, University of Chicago. Fisheries engineer and former business intelligence analyst.

Key takeaways

  • Most of the value of a data tool is created before the chart: defining indicators, cleaning names, linking records to places.
  • Across four projects in three countries, the author built tools for people with very different levels of technical experience.
  • She graduates in June 2027 and is looking for research, policy and data work in Chicago.

The problem

Organizations collect more records than they can use. Outreach logs, loan applications and operational databases often stay as disconnected files because nobody has defined what the numbers mean, who will act on them, and how the tool will be maintained after its builder leaves.

Evidence from four projects

COFI Community Outreach Dashboard

Chicago, summer 2026

An organizing team’s field records became a live neighborhood map of doors knocked, conversations, flyers and referrals. Community names were standardized before mapping, and the system was documented in English and Spanish so staff could maintain it. Open the dashboard

MortgageMatch

UChicago team project, 2026

A dashboard of mortgage approvals and denials across Illinois. As part of the team, the author helped download and clean three public sources, standardize county and lender identifiers, and construct approval, income and loan-size variables. See the repository

Business intelligence in Peru

Teleperformance and Baufest, 2021–2025

SQL extraction, ETL and DAX measures behind dashboards for operational reporting. The recurring lesson: a dashboard is only valuable when its definitions are trustworthy and its audience knows what to do with it.

Jumbo squid surimi research

Universidad Nacional Agraria La Molina, 2024

Thesis research on how a clay-derived additive changes jumbo squid (Dosidicus gigas) surimi gels: experimental design, laboratory measurement and statistical analysis in service of a sustainability question.

Recommendations: what the author brings to a team

  1. Start from the decision, not the dataset.Clarify who needs the analysis and what they should be able to do with it.
  2. Make the data trustworthy before it is pretty.Missing values, inconsistent definitions and duplicates are handled first, in a workflow someone else can trace.
  3. Leave tools others can maintain.Documentation in the language the team works in, English or Spanish.

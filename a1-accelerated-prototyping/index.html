Twenty-five landing pages for one person

I’m Gabriela: fisheries engineer, former business intelligence analyst, and M.S. student in Computational Analysis and Public Policy at the University of Chicago. These are 25 versions of my landing page. I kept all 25 as they were made: each one tests a different idea about who the page is for and how to tell my story.

Each cord is one version, and its knots record the version number in base ten, the way Andean khipus kept records. Brown cords went wide, indigo cords combine, red cords converge. Select any cord to open that version.

Who the page is for

Hiring managers and research leads in Chicago working on policy research, civic tech, environmental data or applied analytics, plus community partners and faculty.

What a visitor should understand

That I turn messy, real-world records into tools people use to decide, and that I have done it in three countries and two languages.

What a visitor should do

Open a project, then get in touch.

The final choice: v25

  1. It says what I do and proves it within one screen: the statement first, then the work.
  2. The khipu is the one bold element. It is specific to me, it encodes real numbers, and it explains how to read itself.
  3. It speaks to both audiences I care about, in both languages I work in.

Why a khipu

The khipu started as one of fifteen experiments in round 1 (v08) and became the signature of my final page. The cords at the top of this gallery are a khipu too: each one records its version number.

What a khipu is

For centuries, record keepers in the Andes stored information on khipus: cords of cotton or camelid fiber hanging from a main cord. Numbers were tied in a base-ten positional system. Clusters of single knots count tens, hundreds and thousands by their height on the cord, a long knot near the end counts units from two to nine by its turns, a figure-eight knot means one, and an empty stretch of cord means zero. Specialists called khipukamayuq made and read them, keeping census counts, tribute and storehouse records for the Inca state.

Why I used it

  • I’m Peruvian, and the khipu is the Andes’ own data technology, used centuries before spreadsheets. My work is about records too: turning messy information into something people can read and use.
  • Every knot is real. The cords on v25 record six true numbers from my work, like 30 dashboards built and 2027, the year I graduate, so the main visual carries data instead of decoration.
  • It makes the page unmistakably mine, which mattered in a class where 28 students each build 25 versions.
  • It’s a small tribute, made with respect: the page teaches visitors how to read the knots instead of using them as ornament.

Round 1: go wide

Fifteen directions, each built from a real artifact of my life instead of a generic style: a research paper, a ship’s log, a BI report, a CTA map, a fish tin, a sonar screen, a khipu, a canvassing sheet. Each one changes the layout, mood and audience, not just colors or fonts.

v01

Research preprint

The page as an academic paper: abstract, methods, a results table and a timeline figure. Credible for research labs, but slow to read, and the projects end up buried in Table 1.

 v02

Ship’s log

My fishing-sailor certificate as the frame: a nautical chart with a dated log of every port and signal flags that spell HOLA. Memorable and true; the log flattens everything into equal rows.

 v03

BI report

A working Power BI-style report with domain slicers, a timeline of roles and a project matrix. It shows the skill instead of describing it, but it feels like work, not like meeting a person.

 v04

The Gabriela Line

A CTA-style system map where science, teaching, data and policy lines converge in Chicago. The strongest Chicago idea; the map takes over the page.

 v05

Conserva label

A vintage Peruvian fish-tin label. Tests humor and Peruvian identity at full volume. Fun, but the jokes compete with the substance.

 v06

Edición bilingüe

A magazine spread with the same profile in Spanish and English, side by side. Shows the bilingual skill, but doubles the reading for everyone.

 v07

Echo sounder

A live echogram where projects show up as fish at depth. The most immersive version; too dark and dense for a recruiter in a hurry.

 v08

Khipu

An interactive khipu: each cord encodes a real number from my work in base-ten knots. Uniquely mine and honest about its data, but it needs a legend to be readable.

 v09

Form GMT-27

A parody government application form. The employment table is genuinely scannable, but the satire risks undercutting me with a policy audience.

 v10

Specimen plate

A Victorian natural-history plate with an engraved jumbo squid. Beautiful, but the antique styling distances the reader from current work.

 v11

Bloques

A block-programming editor whose script runs and makes a squid sprite talk. Right for education partners; too childlike for most hiring managers.

 v12

Walk list

A COFI canvassing sheet on a clipboard, one door per chapter of my story. Warm and specific to organizing; the handwriting slows reading down.

 v13

Single column

A calm page with no decoration at all. The most readable baseline, and the most forgettable.

 v14

Decision path

One question at a time: visitors pick a decision and get the matching evidence. Fast routing to proof, but it adds clicks before anything substantive.

 v15

Descent

A scroll from the surface to 1,000 m with a depth gauge, using the squid’s daily migration as a metaphor. Now in English and Spanish, with a language switch. A long scroll for little content.

What I kept

  • One signature visual that carries real data: the khipu (v08).
  • A credible brief structure and its typography (v17, from v01).
  • Bilingual as a switch, not a duplicate (v06, then v19).
  • Projects before biography (v13).

What I dropped

  • Metaphors that hide the content: the tin, the sonar, the specimen plate (v05, v07, v10).
  • Interfaces that look like tools instead of a person (v03, v11).
  • Gates in front of the evidence (v14).
  • Parallel text in two languages (v06).

Round 2: combine

Five hybrids that pair the strongest ingredients from round 1, to see which combinations survive contact with a real reader.

v16

Khipu index

v13’s calm column with v08’s khipu turned into the table of contents. Identity plus readability works; sideways cords are hard to read.

 v17

Policy brief

v01’s rigor plus v03’s evidence, as a two-column brief with takeaways and small figures. Credible and scannable; its Newsreader and Instrument Sans pairing made it to the final.

 v18

Line timeline

v04’s transit line shrunk to a horizontal timeline over station-sign project cards. A clear path, but everything starts to look like a timeline.

 v19

Language toggle

v06’s bilingual idea as an English/Spanish switch instead of parallel text. Halves the reading load, as long as the switch is easy to find.

 v20

Audience router

v14’s routing as optional chips that reorder v03-style evidence. Helps visitors find relevant proof without putting a gate in front of it.

Round 3: converge

One direction, refined five times: the khipu from v08, the brief from v17, the language switch from v19. Each step fixes the biggest problem left in the one before.

v21

First combination

v08’s khipu in the hero with v17’s brief structure and typography. The khipu takes the whole first screen and the evidence arrives too late.

 v22

Project-first

The hero shrinks to a statement and a compact khipu, and selected work moves directly under it. The takeaways box goes, since it repeated the hero.

 v23

Readable knots

Each cord gets a label, a legend explains the knots, and hovering a cord highlights the project it counts. v20’s audience chips come back.

 v24

Bilingual and accessible

A full English/Spanish toggle from v19, language attributes, keyboard support, reduced motion and a mobile layout.

 v25

Final

The chips are gone because they repeated the work list. The khipu hangs from the header cord, the path becomes one knotted cord of years, and print styles are in.

How I worked with Claude

What I asked for

  • 25 versions of my landing page built only from my own story, my GitHub README and résumé details, so no other student’s page could look like mine.
  • An AGENTS.md that defines who the page is for, what visitors should understand and do, and the technical limits: plain HTML and CSS, no frameworks, no external requests.

What I decided

  • I generated the first drafts in a Claude chat, reviewed all 25, and kept them because each one tests a different idea.
  • I kept my private profile out of the repository, and removed it from the Git history when it slipped in.
  • I chose v25 as my final page, added the khipu explanation to this gallery and made v15 bilingual.
  • I reviewed the Spanish myself, since English and Spanish are both my working languages.

From surimi gels to public policy: turning messy records into decisions people can use

Gabriela Medrano Torres1,2

1 Harris School of Public Policy, University of Chicago    2 Universidad Nacional Agraria La Molina, Lima, Peru

Correspondence: github.com/gmedranotorres

Abstract. This paper reports on one analyst’s attempt to connect three fields that rarely share a desk: fisheries and food science, business intelligence, and computational public policy. Across work in Peru, Spain and the United States, the author built more than 30 Power BI dashboards, a neighborhood outreach map for a Chicago organizing group, part of a team dashboard on Illinois mortgage lending, and thesis research on jumbo squid surimi gels. In each case the decisive step was not the model or the chart but the one before it: defining what the data means and who will act on it. The author completes an M.S. in Computational Analysis and Public Policy in June 2027 and is looking for research, policy and data work in Chicago.

Keywords: administrative data; dashboards; geospatial analysis; environmental policy; bilingual documentation; Dosidicus gigas

1 Introduction

Good decisions depend on reliable data and clear communication. The author began in fisheries engineering at Universidad Nacional Agraria La Molina, finished fourth in her class, and spent an exchange semester and a laboratory internship in food science at Miguel Hernández University in Orihuela, Spain. She then spent nearly four years in business intelligence in Peru before starting graduate study at the University of Chicago in 2025.

The common thread is practical: understand a real problem, organize imperfect evidence, and build something people can use. Her favorite projects “begin with a messy, real-world question and end with a useful, understandable tool.”

2 Methods

Every project in this paper followed the same five steps:

  1. Begin with the decision. Clarify who needs the analysis and what they should be able to do with it.
  2. Examine the data honestly. Look for missing values, inconsistent definitions and duplicates before modeling.
  3. Build a traceable workflow. Organize transformations and assumptions so someone else can follow them.
  4. Challenge the result. Validate outputs and ask whether the evidence supports the interpretation.
  5. Deliver for the audience. A model, notebook, map, dashboard or written explanation, as long as its users can use it.

3 Results

Table 1 summarizes four bodies of work. In Chicago, field outreach records were turned into a live neighborhood map for an organizing team, with community names standardized and the system documented in English and Spanish so staff could maintain it. In a team project, three public datasets were merged into an interactive view of mortgage lending across Illinois. In Lima, the author’s thesis research measured how a clay-derived additive changes texture, gel strength and water-holding capacity in squid surimi. 4 Discussion

Several questions remain open and motivate the author’s next work. How can environmental monitoring data lead to stronger enforcement? Where are public services or community outreach failing to reach the people who need them? How can administrative records become evidence rather than disconnected files? When can machine learning improve targeting while keeping decisions transparent? And how can food and aquatic-resource systems become more sustainable without losing sight of local livelihoods?

The author works in English and Spanish and has taught programming to Spanish-speaking beginners, using block-based tools as a bridge to Python. Explaining a result is treated as part of the result.

5 Conclusion

A dashboard is only valuable when its definitions are trustworthy and its audience knows what to do with the information. The author completes her M.S. in June 2027 and welcomes collaboration with Chicago teams in policy research, civic technology and environmental data. Correspondence via GitHub.
